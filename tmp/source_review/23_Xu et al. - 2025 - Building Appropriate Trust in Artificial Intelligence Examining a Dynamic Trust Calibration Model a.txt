TRUST CALIBRATION IN AI 1
Building Appropriate Trust in Artificial Intelligence: 
Examining a Dynamic Trust Calibration Model and an Intervention
Abstract
This study conceptualizes trust in artificial intelligence as a calibrated response to the system’s 
performance, aimed at fostering appropriate levels of trust in AI. We refine the trust calibration 
model and propose four types of trusting behaviors: under trust, over-trust, validated trust, and 
discerning trust, and examine their antecedents, temporal trajectory, and the impact of 
performance feedback as an intervention strategy. Two experiments assessed the dynamic 
evolution of these trust behaviors across 24 human-AI collaborative tasks. Time-series random-
effects modeling revealed that in both studies, over-trust increased with task difficulty, with 
individuals of higher cognitive ability showing a sharper rise in over-trust on more difficult 
tasks. In Study 1, AI’s chain-of-thought explanations effectively reduced under-trust and 
increased validated trust, though these effects did not hold in Study 2, where a performance 
feedback intervention was introduced. In Study 2, performance feedback helped participants 
reduce over-trust and fostered discerning trust over multiple trials.
Keywords: trust, artificial intelligence, trust calibration, intervention, over-trust, feedback

Building Appropriate Trust in Artificial Intelligence: 
Examining a Dynamic Trust Calibration Model and an Intervention
Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have sparked a growing body of 
research on how users trust these systems. Much of this work draws from classical trust models 
rooted in interpersonal communication and organizational psychology; most notably, Mayer et 
al.’s (1995) integrative model of interpersonal trust, which identifies ability, benevolence, and 
integrity as its core dimensions. This framework has evolved into the widely used distinction 
between cognition-based and affect-based trust: the former emphasizes perceptions of 
competence, while the latter involves emotional bonds and perceived goodwill (e.g., Choung et 
al., 2021). Applied to human-technology interaction, trust is typically conceptualized as an 
attitudinal construct and measured through self-reported strength of trust-related beliefs. Even 
when studies examine trust in a system’s performance, they often rely on perceived rather than 
objectively measured performance (Hoff & Bashir, 2015). As a result, several important gaps 
remain in trust research on AI. Namely, there is limited attention to whether AI systems merit the 
trust they receive, insufficient exploration of the risks of over-trusting malfunctioning AI, and a 
lack of intervention to foster appropriate levels of trust. 
Therefore, this paper aims to advance the literature by shifting the focus from merely 
examining the strength of trust in technologies to examining the calibration of trust in them. That 
is, trust in technologies should be adjusted to reflect the capabilities and limitations of the 
technology (De Visser et al., 2020; Lee & See, 2004). Trust calibration thus adds an additional 
dimension: technology performance, to better understand how human trust fluctuates in response 
to a system’s performance (Klingbeil et al., 2024; Küper & Krämer, 2024; Wischnewski et al., 
2023). Trust calibration is relatively well-established within the human-machine interaction 
community. The first explicit conceptualization of trust calibration was proposed by engineers

The Refined Trust Calibration Model 
In much of the existing literature, trust is conceptualized as the strength of attitudinal 
trust assessed through self-report (Hoff & Bashir, 2015). By contrast, the trust calibration model 
defines trust as a behavioral alignment with a system’s actual performance, reflecting how trust 
operates in real-world decision-making contexts (de Visser et al., 2020). This model also enables 
fine-grained distinctions among different types of trust: under-trust, over-trust, and calibrated 
trust, by organizing them along two key dimensions: AI performance and human decision-
making. Under-trust is defined as the situation in which individuals have lower trust in the 
technology than it deserves when AI performance is high but human trust is low. In situations of 
under-trust, individuals fail to take full advantage of the technology’s capabilities. Over-trust 
occurs when individuals place excessive trust in the technology beyond its actual capabilities (de

disproportionally high. Both under-trust and over-trust are forms of miscalibration of trust and 
can result in detrimental and even dangerous outcomes (e.g., Stafford, 2024). 
It is hoped to bring both under-trust and over-trust to an ideal level of trust, known as 
calibrated trust (de Visser et al., 2020; Lee & See, 2004; Wischnewski et al., 2023). In this state, 
human trust level changes as a function of AI’s capacity (de Visser et al., 2020). By calibrating 
trust, users can more accurately assess the AI’s capabilities and limitations, as to appropriately 
trust or reject the AI’s suggestions based on its actual performance. This calibrated trust allows 
for more effective human-AI collaboration, reduces the risk of relying on technology beyond its 
competency, and enhances adaptive decision-making. 
Importantly, we proposed that under the umbrella concept of calibrated trust, there are 
two distinct types of trust: validated trust and discerning trust. Validated trust occurs when 
human trust levels are high in response to high AI performance, whereas discerning trust occurs 
when human trust levels are low in response to low AI performance. We consider them distinct 
concepts due to their distinct psychological mechanisms. First, humans have a natural tendency 
to save their cognitive efforts and rely on aids in problem solving (Buçinca et al., 2021; 
Hampton, 2005). This tendency may lead to a higher likelihood of validated trust compared to 
discerning trust, and overcoming this trusting tendency is essential for trust calibration. 
Secondly, compared to validated trust, discerning trust requires a higher level of human ability to 
detect AI’s mistakes (Juvina et al., 2019). Developing discerning trust involves training to 
increase individuals’ ability to recognize and respond to AI errors effectively. Thirdly, 
distinguishing between validated trust and discerning trust has important practical implications 
for real-life AI applications. In situations where AI performance is reliably high and stakes are 
relatively low, promoting validated trust can enhance work efficiency, whereas in high-stakes

In sum, the refined trust calibration model advances the AI trust literature in three key 
ways. Conceptually, it defines trust as the alignment between human decision-making and AI 
performance, offering diagnostic insight into whether trust is accurately placed. This contrasts 
with traditional models that treat trust as a subjective psychological state independent of system 
performance. Methodologically, the model prioritizes direct measures of trusting behavior, or 
trust-in-action, which better reflect how trust operates in real-world contexts, unlike self-reported 
attitudes that may not predict behavior or capture dynamic adjustments. Practically, in high-
stakes settings such as healthcare, autonomous vehicles, and hiring, users must not only feel trust 
but act in accordance with system performance. The trust calibration model distinguishes 
between types of trust (over-trust, under-trust, validated trust, and discerning trust), providing 
actionable insights for designing trustworthy systems and informing AI policy. By reframing the 
question from “how much do users say they trust?” to “do users trust AI when it is warranted and 
withhold trust when it is not?”, the model enables more effective and responsible human-AI 
interaction. 
Importantly, we do not view the trust calibration model as a replacement for subjective or 
attitudinal conceptualization of trust, but rather as a necessary and underutilized complement. 
While subjective trust captures users’ internal states, behavioral trust calibration assesses 
whether those perceptions translate into performance-aligned actions. Both perspectives are 
essential for a comprehensive understanding of human-AI interaction. In high-stakes domains 
where safety, fairness, or accuracy matter, an objective benchmark, however imperfectly 
perceived, is critical. Conceptualizing trust as behavioral alignment with actual AI performance

whether individuals can better calibrate their trust over multiple trials.
Task Difficulty 
Empirical evidence has demonstrated task difficulty as one of the most robust 
explanatory variables of trusting AI (Hancock et al., 2011; Kaplan et al., 2023). As a task 
becomes more difficult, the level of ability and the amount of effort required to complete the task 
increases, prompting decision-makers to rely more on decision aids such as AI (Bogert et al., 
2021). Numerous studies support the influence of task difficulty on trusting AI. For example, in a

system’s output or not (Leichtmann et al., 2023). This, in turn, increases the likelihood of making 
correct decisions by analyzing both accurate and inaccurate AI outputs, thereby achieving 
validated trust and discerning trust while reducing over-trust and under-trust. However, the

increase calibrated trust (validated trust and discerning trust) and reduce over-trust. Yet, other

explanations as a sign of competence, leading them to agree with the AI even when the 
explanation is incorrect (Buçinca et al., 2021).
 We speculate that the inconsistent results regarding explanations may be due to the 
different types of explanations that AI provides. We aim to advance research on explainable AI 
(XAI) by distinguishing between the effects of chain-of-thought (CoT) explanations and 
technical explanations. In line with Saeed and Omlin (2023), we conceptualize explainable AI as 
the development of techniques that empower end-users to comprehend, trust, and effectively 
manage AI systems. This user-centered perspective of XAI emphasizes interpretability and 
comprehensibility of AI output over technical transparency. In our study, we focus on output-
level explanations; specifically, CoT and technical explanations, as practical tools for supporting 
user understanding of AI-generated outputs. CoT explanations are generated outputs that emulate 
human reasoning by laying out the intermediate steps between a problem statement and its final 
solution using natural, conversational language (Wei et al., 2022). Importantly, this differs 
from CoT prompting, which refers to the input technique used to elicit such reasoning from AI 
models; CoT explanations, by contrast, are the outputs users receive. CoT reasoning has not only

chain-strategy of human reasoning identified in cognitive psychology (Sloman, 1996; Johnson-

Thus, CoT explanations differ from more technical explanations in their user-friendly 
interpretability. Technical explanations typically use formal mathematical or coding language, 
such as numbers, equations, and statistics. For example, a technical explanation might state, “The 
suggested answer is 15: 7+8=15.” In contrast, CoT explanations provide explicit logical 
connections in human language. For instance, “The suggested answer is 15: to find out the total 
number of people in the room, we combine 7 females and 8 males: 7+8=15.” Current popular 
generative AI assistants, such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Llama, offer a mix of CoT 
explanations and technical explanations, depending on the user prompts. To the best of our 
knowledge, this is probably one of the first empirical studies examining the effect of CoT 
explanations on the four types of trust. Therefore, we propose the following research question.
RQ1: Will the chain-of-thought explanation provided by AI increase the likelihood of (a) 
validated trust and (b) discerning trust, and reduce (c) over-trust and (d) under-trust?
Trust Calibration as a Dynamic Process 
Trust calibration is a dynamic process that evolves with each interaction between the user 
and the AI assistant, gradually building on past experiences. Users may begin with a certain 
initial level of trust, but this trust is continuously updated through ongoing interactions (Juvina et 
al., 2019). Over time, this trial-and-error process enables users to learn how to effectively 
collaborate with AI and determine the extent to which they should trust it, ultimately enhancing 
task performance in the long run (Wang, 2014). Therefore, time plays a critical role in the

development of trust levels in human-AI collaboration. Despite long-standing calls for an over-
time approach to examine how trust calibration develops and adjusts over time (de Visser et al., 
2020; Juvina et al., 2019; Wischnewski et al., 2023), few investigations have addressed its 
temporal dimension. This scarcity is partly due to the prevalent use of experimental designs and 
analytical approaches that summarize averaged differences across multiple interactions and miss 
the evolving picture of trust calibration. 
The current study aims to capture the dynamics of the four types of trust over time by 
incorporating time as a key predictor in the trust development process (Wang, 2014). Instead of 
assessing trust only at the end of multiple interactions, we evaluate trust at each interaction point, 
offering a more nuanced perspective and accurate estimation of trust in AI. This approach moves 
beyond the static conception of trust that is prevalent in much of the existing empirical research 
(Wischnewski et al., 2023). While recent studies and technical reports have begun to explore 
how human trust evolves over time (e.g., Chen, 2022; Zafari et al., 2024),these investigations 
primarily rely on self-reported general trust in AI and do not contribute significantly to 
understanding the learned process of trust calibration. This study is one of the first to analyze 
how the four types of trust in the trust calibration model—validated trust, discerning trust, over-
trust, and under-trust—develop over time.
RQ2: How do the four types of trust: (a) validated trust, (b) discerning trust, (c) over-
trust, and (d) under-trust, develop over the course of multiple trials?

(Wischnewski et al., 2023). Feedback3 confirms the accuracy or inaccuracy of human decisions 
in various tasks (de Visser et al., 2016). Since trust calibration is developed in uncertain 
situations, receiving feedback on the correctness of AI-assisted human decisions provides more 
information to reduce uncertainty, and can help individuals better assess the reliability of the AI 
(de Visser et al., 2016). This feedback process thus can potentially increase validated trust and 
discerning trust while decreasing under-trust and over-trust in AI.
A small number of existing studies have found mixed effects of performance feedback on 
users’ trust in AI. For example, Dzindolet et al., (2003) observed that participants who received 
feedback on automation performance exhibited moderated levels of trust in automated assistance. 
Similarly, experiments by de Visser et al. (2016) demonstrated that immediate feedback on task 
performance reduced participants’ general trust levels in the AI assistant. In contrast, studies 
specifically examining trust calibration have produced conflicting results. Lu and Sarter (2019) 
found that performance feedback did not significantly impact trust calibration, while Merritt and 
colleagues (2015) reported positive effects of feedback on fostering well-aligned levels trust in 
an automated assistant. In the current study, we aim to explore whether providing feedback can 
help individuals adjust and calibrate their trust more accurately over time. Specifically, we seek 
to determine if feedback can increase validated trust and discerning trust while reducing under-
trust and over-trust over-time, an area where empirical evidence remains inconclusive.
RQ3: When receiving performance feedback for each task, will individuals develop more 
(a) validated and (b) discerning trust, and less (c) over-trust and (d) under-trust, over the course 
of multiple trials?

Methods
We conducted two experimental studies (Study 1 and Study 2) to test the proposed 
hypotheses and research questions. In addition to replicating the hypotheses and research 
questions in Study 1, Study 2 is also designed to test the effects of feedback as an intervention 
strategy (RQ3) by comparing the results from both studies. To ensure the comparability of 
Studies 1 and 2, data for both were collected simultaneously, with participants randomly 
assigned to either study, as recommended by Arendt (2023). The random assignment of 
participants ensures that the sample populations for both studies are comparable.
Study 1
Participants. The study included 362 U.S. residents aged 19 to 85 years (M = 43.84, SD 
= 14.70; 47.51% male), based on quota-sampling techniques (gender and ethnicity). Ethnic 
composition was 58.84% White, 17.13% Black, 19.06% Hispanic, and 8.84% Asian. Participants 
were recruited via Prolific (Douglas et al., 2023) and received a base payment of $3, with an 
additional $1 incentive for the top 30% based on performance. The number of participants was 
calculated using G*Power 3.1 for a mixed ANOVA: repeated measures, within-between 
interaction. Assuming a small effect size for the CoT explanation (f = 0.10), α = 0.05, and power 
= 0.80, the analysis indicated a required sample size of 200 participants. However, considering 
the web-based nature of this online experiment, we anticipated challenges such as higher dropout 
rates, inattentiveness, incomplete responses, and environmental variability. To ensure sufficient 
usable data and maintain statistical power, we increased the recruitment target to 350-400 
participants, consistent with practices in comparable online experimental studies (e.g., Arendt, 
2023)

Discussion
This study calls for a shift in trust research from focusing solely on perceived trust in AI 
to examining how well trust is calibrated to reflect actual system performance. This reframing is 
critical for several reasons. First, it reconceptualizes trust as the alignment between human 
decision-making and AI reliability, offering diagnostic clarity on whether trust is appropriately 
placed, unlike traditional models that treat trust primarily as a subjective state. Second, it 
emphasizes direct behavioral measures of trust, capturing how trust manifests in real-world 
decisions more effectively than static self-report measures. Third, practically in high-stakes 
domains such as healthcare, autonomous systems, and employment, the consequences of 
misaligned trust can be severe; what matters is not just whether users feel trust, but whether they 
act in line with the AI’s actual capabilities. Our refined trust calibration model further 
distinguishes among trust types (over-trust, under-trust, validated trust, discerning trust), offering 
a more theoretical nuances and practical insights for system design and AI governance. By 
shifting the central question from “how much trust do users feel/report?” to “is trust 
appropriately placed given AI performance?”, this model advances a more accountable and 
effective framework for human-AI interaction.
While we recognize that the public is still in the early stages of knowing, accepting, and 
adopting AI technologies (Xu et al., 2023), it is imperative for scientists to anticipate future 
challenges, and adopt a more cautious and forward-looking approach. This should not only 
address the issues of under-trust in AI at present, but also prevent the potential for over-trust 
once these technologies are widely adopted. Furthermore, developing intervention strategies to

Heuristic Values of the Refined Trust Calibration Model
We hope that the model’s fine-grained distinctions between different types of trust (e.g., 
over-trust, under-trust, validated trust, and discerning trust) may offer a useful heuristic 
framework for organizing and reconciling seemingly contradictory findings in the human-AI 
interaction literature. For instance, some studies have documented a tendency toward machine 
heuristics and over-trust in AI systems (Sundar & Kim, 2019), while others have highlighted 
algorithm aversion and under-trust (Dietvorst et al., 2015), as well as a lack of human agency 
(Sundar, 2020). A recent meta-analysis further complicates the landscape by showing that AI 
agents are often perceived as less persuasive than human counterparts (Huang & Wang, 2023). 
Rather than treating these findings as inconsistent, the trust calibration model may provide a 
unifying lens by conceptualizing trust as a dynamic alignment between user judgment and 
system performance. In doing so, the model may serve as a conceptual bridge for testing 
competing hypotheses and synthesizing insights across diverse strands of scholarship. 
Furthermore, the model invites renewed attention to the role of social responses to AI 
grounded in the Computers as Social Actors (CASA) framework (Nass & Moon, 2000). When 
users treat AI agents as social entities, applying interpersonal norms and expectations, such 
tendencies may unintentionally erode discerning trust and foster over-trust (Culley & Madhavan, 
2013). Understanding how these socially conditioned responses interact with trust calibration 
processes represents a promising direction for future research.

A key theoretical contribution of this study lies in its conceptual refinement and empirical 
testing of four distinct types of trust behaviors in human-AI interaction: under-trust, over-

behaviors manifest in everyday contexts, how they may evolve into stable individual dispositions

Practical Implications
Human-AI collaboration is increasingly likely to be implemented in organizations due to 
the growing need to enhance efficiency, decision-making, and productivity. However, the 
effectiveness of employee-AI collaboration heavily depends on the calibration of trust between 
users and AI systems. Our findings highlight the effectiveness of performance feedback in 
increasing discerning trust and reducing over-trust in AI. While providing constant feedback for 
every human-AI collaborative task at work may be unrealistic, organizations deploying AI tools 
should integrate structured feedback mechanisms into their employee training programs. 
Training workshops should be designed to include performance feedback sessions, allowing 
employees to calibrate their trust in AI system via various mock tasks. This will help employees 
develop appropriate trust levels, avoiding both underutilization and over-reliance on AI systems. 
Moreover, our study underscores the importance of cognitive abilities, particularly 
numeracy, in fostering discerning trust and reducing over-trust in AI systems. Numeracy is 
essential for critically evaluating AI outputs, identifying errors, and making informed decisions. 
Knowledge of probability, matrices, and calculus helps users understand AI algorithms, 
predictions, and AI decision-making processes. Therefore, educational systems should prioritize 
math and numeracy skills to improve mathematical proficiency and prepare the future workforce 
with the analytical abilities required for effective and responsible interaction with AI systems. 
For AI system design and deployment, the identification of numeracy as a moderating factor has

Limitations and Future Research
As discussed earlier, this study utilized behavioral measures to operationalize the four 
types of trust in the refined trust calibration model. While this approach is valuable and 
complement the existing widely used self-report measure, it is not the only or necessarily the best 
method to assess trust calibration (Wischnewski et al., 2024). Future research should consider 
using a combination of self-report and behavioral measures of trust in experiments, and 
developing survey instruments or alternative methodologies to assess these trust types in real-
world settings. Examining how calibrated trust evolves over time in naturalistic contexts can 
address the ecological validity issue often present in experimental designs, where the forced 
usage of AI may lead to negative reactions including frustration or resistance (Karimikia et al., 
2020). This study employs an AI assistant with a fixed accuracy rate of 50%. However, future 
research could explore how trust types fluctuate for AI assistants with varying accuracy levels to 
identify the threshold accuracy rate that influences trust calibration and its trends over time.
This paper does not have sufficient space to fully hypothesize the discriminant validity 
between validated trust and discerning trust. However, the proposed differences between them 
are supported by the results from both Study 1 and Study 2, where validated trust occurred 
significantly more often than discerning trust (Study 1: t = 8.82, p <.001; Study 2: t = 10.50, p 
<.001). Cognitive ability significantly predicted discerning trust (Study 1: t = 6.50, p <.001; 
Study 2: t = 6.50, p <.001), but not validated trust (see Tables 1 and 2), indicating that discerning 
trust is more challenging to achieve. Future research should continue to investigate the

incorporate a broader range of cognitive measures to examine whether different dimensions of

increased validated trust in Study 1, we cannot entirely rule out the influence of length as a 
confounding factor. Future research should systematically manipulate explanation length and 
CoT explanation independently to better isolate their respective effects and clarify the causal 
mechanisms underlying explanation effectiveness in AI-human collaboration.

testing four distinct types of trust behaviors: validated trust, discerning trust, over-trust, and 
under-trust, in human-AI collaboration. Through two experimental studies, we demonstrate how 
individual differences, task characteristics, and intervention strategies jointly shape trust 
trajectories across multiple interactions. Our findings highlight the diagnostic value of 
distinguishing among trust types, offering a more behaviorally grounded and theoretically 
precise account of how users respond to AI outputs. Importantly, we show that cognitive ability,

adaptive, user-centered interventions. As AI systems continue to evolve and permeate daily life, 
promoting appropriate trust: trust that is neither blindly given nor unfairly withheld, will be 
critical to ensuring ethical, effective, and responsible human-AI collaboration.
