1. Introduction and Motivation
Large Language Models (LLMs) have rapidly evolved from experimental research systems
into integral components of modern software engineering workflows. Tools such as OpenAI
ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot, and CodeGeeX are now widely used by developers for tasks including
code generation, debugging, documentation, refactoring, testing, and software maintenance.
Recent studies indicate that 77% of developers use ChatGPT and 46% use GitHub Copilot
during development activities Borg, 2024. Among specialist practitioners, nearly all respondents
reported using LLMs for software engineering tasks on at least a weekly basis Khati et al.,
2025. The integration of LLMs into development environments has fundamentally changed how
software is produced. Industry reports suggest that, in some contexts, more than half of newly
written code is generated with AI assistance Spiess et al., 2024. Modern LLM-based coding
assistants support a wide range of activities across the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC),
including automated code completion, test generation, API usage assistance, large-scale code
migration, and architectural recommendations Ishaani M et al., 2024. As a result, AI-assisted
development is transitioning from optional productivity support to a core element of professional
software engineering practice.
The rapid adoption of LLMs is largely driven by their potential to improve developer productivity.
Prior research has shown that developers using GitHub Copilot completed programming tasks
approximately 55.8% faster than developers working without AI assistance Khati et al., 2025. By
automating repetitive coding patterns, reducing syntax recall effort, and accelerating information
retrieval, LLMs enable developers to focus more on higher-level problem solving and system
design.
However, the productivity benefits of LLMs coexist with significant reliability concerns. Despite
impressive generative capabilities, current models frequently produce inaccurate, insecure, or non-
functional outputs. Studies have shown that state-of-the-art LLMs generate correct refactorings
in only a subset of cases, while some automatically generated modifications introduce syntax
errors or break existing functionality Borg, 2024; Khati et al., 2025. This creates a critical tension
within AI-assisted software engineering: developers experience substantial productivity gains
while simultaneously facing increased risks related to correctness, maintainability, and security.
The primary challenge is not simply that LLMs make mistakes, but that developers often struggle
to accurately judge when AI-generated outputs are trustworthy. This phenomenon is known
astrust miscalibrationBorg, 2024; Khati et al., 2025, a mismatch between the level of trust
a developer places in AI-generated content and the actual reliability of that content. Trust
miscalibration manifests in two major forms.Over-trustoccurs when developers accept AI-
generated suggestions without sufficient verification, potentially introducing subtle bugs, security
vulnerabilities, or architectural flaws into software systems. Prior research has documented cases
where developers unknowingly accepted insecure code recommendations or spent significant
time debugging hallucinated libraries and non-existent APIs suggested by LLMs Barke et al.,
2022; Ishaani M et al., 2024. As AI-generated code becomes increasingly prevalent in production
environments, such failures can scale into broader software reliability and security risks. And
under-trustoccurs when developers lose confidence in AI systems after observing errors, causing
them to underutilize otherwise valuable tools. Excessive skepticism may reduce productivity
gains and prevent effective human-AI collaboration. Therefore, the challenge is not whether
developers should trust LLMs, but rather how much trust is appropriate in different development
situations.
Trust calibration refers to aligning a developer’s level of reliance on AI-generated output with

the actual reliability of that output. In the context of AI-assisted software engineering, trust
calibration is increasingly recognized as a critical prerequisite for safe and effective LLM
adoption Khati et al., 2025.
Well-calibrated trust enables developers to appropriately adjust their verification behavior based
on the perceived confidence and risk associated with AI suggestions. High-confidence and low-
risk outputs may require only lightweight review, whereas uncertain or high-impact code should
trigger deeper inspection and validation. Such adaptive behavior supports both productivity and
software quality. Poorly calibrated trust can lead either to reckless dependence on unreliable
outputs or unnecessary rejection of useful assistance. As LLMs become embedded in professional
development environments, understanding how developers form, adjust, and maintain trust in AI
systems becomes an increasingly important research problem.
Although existing research has explored the accuracy and usability of LLMs in software en-
gineering, comparatively less attention has been given to the cognitive processes that shape
developer trust during AI-assisted programming. Developers interact with LLMs under varying
cognitive conditions, such as time pressure, multitasking, familiarity with technologies, and
differing levels of expertise that may influence how critically they evaluate AI-generated outputs.
This research is motivated by the need to better understand how trust miscalibration emerges
across different cognitive modes during LLM-assisted software engineering tasks. While existing
studies have examined AI accuracy, productivity, and general trust behavior, limited attention
has been given to how developers’ cognitive states influence their reliance on AI-generated
suggestions. Developers do not interact with LLMs uniformly; their verification behavior,
attention levels, and decision-making processes change depending on whether they are working
in fast-paced routine tasks or deliberate problem-solving situations. This study therefore focuses
on understanding the relationship between developer cognition, trust formation, and AI-assisted
programming behavior. In particular, it aims to investigate how trust is formed, maintained,
and miscalibrated across different interaction modes, and how these modes affect developers’
ability to critically evaluate AI-generated code. By empirically examining these behaviors,
the research seeks to contribute toward a deeper understanding of human-AI collaboration in
software engineering and the conditions under which reliable oversight succeeds or fails.
2. Background and Preliminary Literature Review
2.1 Trust in Human-AI Interaction
Trust is commonly defined as “an attitude that an agent will help achieve an individual’s goals in
a situation characterized by uncertainty and vulnerability” Lee and See, 2004. Within human-AI
interaction, trust represents a developer’s subjective belief in the reliability and usefulness of
an AI system. This differs fromtrustworthiness, which refers to the system’s actual capability
to perform correctly and consistently. The mismatch between perceived reliability and actual
reliability is known astrust miscalibration. In the context of software engineering, this distinction
is especially important because developers often rely on probabilistic outputs generated by LLMs
without having full visibility into their correctness. Spiess et al. (2024) demonstrated this gap by
showing that completions with 91% per-token confidence passed test cases only 52% of the time.
This illustrates that model confidence alone is not a reliable indicator of correctness.
Trust is also dynamic rather than static. Developers continuously update their trust in AI systems
based on prior interactions, successful completions, observed failures, and task context. As
developers accumulate experience with AI-assisted programming tools, their reliance behavior
evolves over time, making trust calibration an ongoing cognitive process rather than a one-time

decision.
Several theoretical frameworks help explain how developers build trust in AI-assisted program-
ming tools and why that trust can sometimes become miscalibrated. Lee and See (2004) provide
the foundational understanding of trust by distinguishing between a developer’s belief in the
AI system and the system’s actual reliability. Their work forms the basis for much of the later
research on trust calibration in software engineering. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) further
explain that frequent interaction with automated systems can reduce user vigilance over time,
causing people to rely on automation without carefully checking its outputs. This idea is closely
related to automation bias in AI-assisted coding. From a cognitive perspective, Kahneman
(2011)’s Dual-Process Theory helps explain how developers think differently depending on
the situation. System 1 thinking is fast and intuitive, leading developers to quickly accept
suggestions during familiar tasks, while System 2 thinking is slower and more analytical, en-
couraging deliberate evaluation during unfamiliar or complex problems. In addition, Okamura
and Yamada (2020) propose that trust is not static and should be continuously adjusted based on
user behavior and system performance. Although their framework was not designed specifically
for software engineering, it provides useful insight into how trust in AI coding assistants may
change dynamically during development tasks.
2.2 Cognitive Modes and the Grounded Copilot Theory
Among existing theories, the most directly relevant to this research is theGrounded Copilot
Theoryproposed by Barke et al. (2022) Barke et al., 2022. Through grounded theory analysis
involving 20 participants using GitHub Copilot across multiple programming languages, the
study identified that developers interact with AI coding assistants through two distinct cognitive
modes:accelerationandexploration.
InAcceleration Mode, developers already possess a clear understanding of the task and use
the AI primarily as an intelligent autocomplete mechanism. Suggestions are accepted rapidly
through pattern recognition rather than detailed analysis. The objective is to preserve cognitive
flow and reduce implementation effort. Prompting in this mode is largely implicit, as completions
appear naturally during typing, and suggestion acceptance latency tends to be extremely short.
For example, Barke et al. (2022) observed participants rapidly accepting multiple suggestions
while implementing familiar functions because the AI merely accelerated a solution they already
understood.
In contrast,Exploration Modeoccurs when developers face uncertainty, such as unfamiliar
APIs, new libraries, complex debugging situations, or unfamiliar problem domains. Here, AI
systems are used not simply for acceleration but for discovery and reasoning support. Developers
intentionally craft prompts, compare alternative suggestions, and validate outputs more carefully
through experimentation and testing.
Importantly, developers do not remain fixed in a single mode. Barke et al. (2022) found that
developers dynamically switch between acceleration and exploration depending on task famil-
iarity and situational uncertainty. A developer may rapidly accept suggestions during familiar
implementation work but transition into deliberate exploratory behavior when encountering
unfamiliar technologies or unexpected problems. This dynamic mode switching is central to
understanding how trust behaviors change during AI-assisted programming.

2.3 Existing Empirical Studies
Several empirical studies have examined trust, reliance, and behavioral interaction patterns in
LLM-assisted software engineering.
Barke et al. (2022) established the bimodal interaction model and demonstrated that verification
behavior changes depending on whether developers are operating in acceleration or exploration
mode. The study also identified early signs of over-reliance among first-time users.
Brown et al. (2024), through large-scale telemetry analysis involving one million AI suggestions
across 59,000 developers, found that language expertise was the strongest predictor of suggestion
acceptance. Developers were also less likely to accept suggestions in sensitive contexts such as
test files or minor code modifications.
Ishaani M et al. (2024) investigated AI-assisted Java migration tasks using Amazon Q. Their
findings showed that developers evaluated AI systems similarly to human teammates, expecting
reliable and contextually accurate assistance. Hallucinated dependencies and non-existent
libraries created what participants described as “gaslighting” experiences, while successful test
execution became a primary indicator of trustworthiness.
Khati et al. (2025) explored practitioner trust perceptions across different software engineering
tasks. Trust levels varied significantly by task type, with developers expressing higher trust in
AI-generated tests than in AI-generated code repair or implementation logic. The majority of
practitioners also reported regularly reviewing AI-generated outputs before acceptance.
Spiess et al. (2024) focused on statistical calibration properties of LLMs across software engi-
neering tasks. Their analysis revealed substantial gaps between model confidence and actual
correctness, highlighting significant Expected Calibration Error (ECE) values across multiple
development contexts.
Finally, Borg (2024) demonstrated that current LLM systems still struggle with reliability in
complex software engineering tasks such as refactoring. The study argued that trust miscali-
bration contributes both to unsafe over-reliance and to delayed adoption caused by excessive
skepticism.
Collectively, these studies show that trust in AI-assisted programming is influenced by expertise,
task type, cognitive behavior, and system reliability. However, despite growing research on
both trust calibration and cognitive interaction patterns, limited work has directly examined how
different cognitive modes influence trust miscalibration during software development activities.
3. Research Gap Analysis
The proposed research gap is:“Understanding Trust Miscalibration Across Developer Cognitive
Modes in LLM-Assisted Software Engineering. ”
This gap is valid for several reasons. First, existing studies on cognitive modes and studies on
trust calibration have mostly been conducted separately, with little integration between them.
Second, many theories suggest that developers become less attentive during fast-paced coding
flow, but this has not been directly measured in software engineering contexts. Finally, current
studies often rely on indirect indicators of trust behavior rather than measuring developers’ actual
cognitive states during AI-assisted programming. Therefore, there is still limited empirical
understanding of how different cognitive modes affect developer trust and verification behavior
when working with LLM-based coding assistants.

4. Research Problem Formulation
During LLM-assisted programming, trust problems arise differently depending on the devel-
oper’s cognitive state. In acceleration mode, developers work in a fast, fluent flow where AI
suggestions are accepted quickly through pattern recognition rather than careful analysis. This
can reduce the natural “pause-and-check” behavior, allowing subtle bugs, security issues, or
incorrect API suggestions to slip through. In exploration mode, the challenge shifts to developers
facing uncertainty and relying more heavily on the AI to guide them, but they may still accept
suggestions they cannot fully verify, sometimes leading to overconfidence and extra debugging
work later. In both situations, trust is not a fully conscious decision but something shaped by the
developer’s mental state and the design of the tool itself, and this often does not match the actual
reliability of the AI.
Current AI coding tools mainly focus on smooth and frictionless interaction, which encourages
high usage but does not ensure correct trust behavior. Simple confidence indicators or warnings
are often ignored or become background noise over time, and existing tools do not adapt to
whether a developer is in a fast execution state or a slow reasoning state. Because of this, it
becomes important to understand which cognitive mode leads to more trust errors and what
signals can identify those modes.
5. Potential Research Questions
RQ1:Do developers accept AI suggestions more quickly and without checking in acceleration
mode compared to exploration mode?
RQ2:How does a developer’s confidence in an AI suggestion compare to whether the code is
actually correct, and does this difference change between the two modes?
RQ3:What kinds of checking or testing do developers do in each mode, and how does this
affect the number of mistakes they accept?
RQ4:Can we identify whether a developer is in fast mode or exploration mode by looking at
IDE signals like how fast they accept suggestions, how they prompt the AI, or how often they
edit code?
RQ5:Do trust miscalibration patterns differ across SE task types (code completion, API
exploration, bug repair) when controlling for cognitive mode?
6. Significance of the Research
This research is significant because it investigates how developers’ trust in AI-assisted coding
tools can affect software quality and decision-making during programming tasks. Existing
studies show that developers may become overly dependent on AI-generated suggestions, even
during simple coding activities, which can increase the risk of unnoticed errors and insecure code
Spiess et al., 2024; Khati et al., 2025 . Research also suggests that this problem becomes stronger
during fast “acceleration” working modes, where developers focus more on speed and flow rather
than careful verification Barke et al., 2022. This behavior is linked to automation bias, where
developers rely on AI recommendations instead of actively checking correctness Parasuraman
and Manzey, 2010. By studying these behaviors, this research can contribute to improving
responsible AI usage in software engineering, designing better AI-assisted development tools,
and helping developers build stronger verification habits when working with AI systems.

7. Possible Methodology
7.1 Study Design
This study will adopt a mixed-methods within-subject controlled experimental design, com-
bining behavioral logging, screen recording, questionnaires, and semi-structured interviews to
investigate how different AI-assisted coding modes influence developer trust calibration and
verification behavior. This mirrors the approaches of Barke et al. (2022) and Ishaani M et al.
(2024), scaled to undergraduate scope.
7.2 Participants
The study will involve approximately 30 participants, including undergraduate Computer Science
and Software Engineering students, early-career developers, and senior software engineers.
The focus is on comparing how programmers with different levels of experience interact with
AI-assisted coding tools, particularly in terms of trust, verification behavior, and reliance on AI-
generated suggestions. Novice and intermediate developers may be more vulnerable to incorrect
AI suggestions due to limited experience, while senior engineers can provide insight into how
professional expertise influences trust calibration and verification practices. This participant
group is important because it reflects the diverse range of developers who are increasingly using
AI tools in real-world software engineering environments.
7.3 Tasks
Phase 1 - Acceleration induction:A familiar algorithm or utility (e.g., basic file parser, data
structure function). The participant already knows the approach; the AI serves as faster execution.
This should reliably produce rapid acceptance and low prompting effort.
Phase 2 - Exploration induction:A task involving an unfamiliar library or API (e.g., data
visualization with Plotly, HTTP requests with a specific library). The participant must rely on
the AI to discover the correct approach. This should produce explicit prompting, suggestion
comparison, and deliberate validation.
A subset of AI suggestions should contain pre-designed, subtle errors (incorrect API usage, logic
bugs) to enable direct measurement of error detection rates across modes.
7.4 Data Collection
Data will be collected using a combination of qualitative and quantitative methods to capture both
behavior and reasoning during AI-assisted coding tasks. A think-aloud protocol will be used,
where participants verbalize their thought process while their audio, video, and screen activity
are recorded to understand cognitive and verification behavior. In addition, a lightweight VS
Code extension will log interaction data such as AI suggestion acceptance and rejection events,
response latency, and edit distance to measure how much participants modify generated code.
During the tasks, participants will also provide confidence ratings using a short Likert scale at key
decision points, allowing comparison between perceived confidence and actual correctness. After
each task, participants will label parts of their experience as either “in the zone” (acceleration) or
“figuring it out” (exploration), which will be compared with researcher observations. Finally,
short post-task interviews will be conducted to explore participants’ reasoning, trust in AI
suggestions, moments of uncertainty, and factors influencing their decisions.
7.5 Ethical Considerations
All participants will take part voluntarily and will provide informed consent before the study
begins. Participants will be informed about the purpose of the research, the types of data being

8. Scope and Delimitations
8.1 Include
• Behavioral study of developer interaction (not model evaluation)
• Two-phase tasks inducing both cognitive modes
• Mixed-method data collection
• Undergraduate and industrial participants
• Analysis across at least two SE task types
8.2 Exclude
• Model training or fine-tuning (use off-the-shelf tools only)
• Large-scale telemetry processing (depth over breadth)
• Adaptive IDE system development (this study provides the foundation for that work, not the
work itself)
• Longitudinal study design
